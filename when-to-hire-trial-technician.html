Trial technology guide

When should defense counsel bring in a trial technician?

The short answer: before trial preparation becomes a scramble. A trial technician is most valuable when there is enough time to understand the case—not merely load the exhibits.

Waiting until the courthouse doors are about to open turns a potentially strategic role into a reactive technical role. Earlier involvement lets the presentation system develop alongside the trial plan.

Four signs it is time to bring someone in

1. The exhibit universe is becoming difficult to navigate.

If the team has hundreds or thousands of documents, images, medical records, videos, or demonstratives, speed requires organization before court. A useful trial database should reflect how counsel thinks about witnesses and issues, not merely file names.

2. Deposition video will matter.

Designations, counter-designations, objections, synchronized transcripts, and impeachment clips can become a substantial workflow. Preparing them early reduces last-minute editing and makes prior testimony easier to use during examination.

3. The case depends on visual explanation.

Medical causation, construction sequences, product mechanics, timelines, financial evidence, and competing damages models are often easier to understand when the presentation strategy is developed before opening statement.

4. Impeachment needs to be immediate.

The power of a prior statement can disappear if the courtroom waits while someone searches for it. A trial technician can prepare fast-access testimony and exhibit workflows around likely credibility issues.

What early involvement should accomplish

The goal is not to add another person to meetings. It is to make the eventual courtroom presentation more predictable. By trial, the team should know how exhibits will be called up, how video will play, how demonstratives will appear, how impeachment will be retrieved, and what backup exists if equipment fails.

The distinction that matters

A courtroom AV provider can make screens and equipment work. A defense trial technician or hot-seat operator works with the litigation team to make the evidence work in the presentation of the case.
