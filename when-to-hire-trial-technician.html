Trial technology guide for defense counsel

When should defense counsel hire a trial technician?

The short answer: before trial preparation becomes a scramble. A trial technician is most valuable when there is enough time to understand the case, organize the evidence, and build a presentation system around counsel’s trial plan—not merely load files at the courthouse.

The best time to hire a trial technician depends less on the calendar than on the complexity of the presentation. Exhibit volume, deposition video, demonstratives, impeachment material, courtroom logistics, and the number of witnesses all affect how much preparation is required.

For a short trial with a modest exhibit set, the final few weeks may provide enough time. For a document-heavy, video-intensive, or technically complex matter, involving the technician earlier can prevent avoidable rework and give the litigation team time to test how the case will actually be presented.

The practical rule: hire before presentation decisions become irreversible

A technician brought in on the eve of trial can still operate software and display evidence. But late involvement often limits the role to reacting to whatever organization, naming conventions, video edits, and demonstratives already exist.

Earlier involvement creates an opportunity to identify missing files, inconsistent exhibit versions, incomplete deposition designations, incompatible media, unclear witness folders, and courtroom technology limitations while there is still time to address them. It also allows the technician to learn how lead counsel wants to move through examinations and argument.

Six signs it is time to bring in a trial technician

1. The exhibit universe is becoming difficult to navigate

If the team has hundreds or thousands of documents, photographs, medical records, spreadsheets, videos, or demonstratives, speed in court requires structure before trial. A useful presentation database should reflect witnesses, issues, and likely sequences—not merely the file names received in discovery.

When several attorneys or vendors are maintaining separate versions, early coordination also helps establish which files are final and who controls updates.

2. Deposition video will play a meaningful role

Video designations can create a substantial workflow: collecting source files, synchronizing transcripts, applying rulings, tracking counter-designations, editing clips, checking audio, and organizing playback by witness and issue.

If deposition testimony will be used in the case-in-chief or for impeachment, the technician should have enough time to verify that every clip plays correctly and matches the controlling designation list.

3. The case depends on visual explanation

Medical causation, construction sequences, product mechanics, timelines, financial evidence, and competing damages models are often easier to understand visually. In those cases, trial technology and litigation graphics should be considered together.

Early collaboration helps the team decide which concepts need a demonstrative, which evidence should be shown side by side, and how visuals will transition during testimony and argument.

4. Impeachment needs to be immediate

The force of a prior statement can weaken if the courtroom waits while the team searches for it. A trial technician can organize transcripts, clips, exhibits, and callouts around likely credibility disputes so counsel can retrieve the right material without losing momentum.

5. Multiple lawyers will present from the same evidence set

Different attorneys often develop different habits for requesting exhibits and moving through examinations. A shared presentation protocol—combined with witness-specific preparation—reduces confusion and helps the technician anticipate what each attorney is likely to need next.

6. The venue has uncertain or limited courtroom technology

Courtroom capabilities vary. Display inputs, audio routing, evidence monitors, network access, sightlines, placement restrictions, and permission to bring outside equipment should be confirmed before trial. When the venue cannot support the presentation plan, the team needs time to develop a reliable alternative and backup.

When to involve the technician during the life of the case

During expert and dispositive-motion preparation

Not every case needs technical support this early. But if expert testimony depends on animation, layered diagrams, large data sets, or complex tutorials, early consultation can help preserve consistency between reports, demonstratives, and the eventual trial presentation.

When the trial date becomes firm

This is often the ideal point for an initial presentation assessment. The technician can estimate the work involved, review venue requirements, identify the likely equipment package, and help the team establish deadlines for exhibits, video, and demonstratives.

During final pretrial preparation

By this stage, the technician should be building and testing the working trial database, processing updated exhibits, preparing video, coordinating graphics, and rehearsing important sequences with counsel.

At the courthouse

Court should be the execution phase, not the first time the system is assembled. The presentation workflow, primary equipment, backups, exhibit organization, and communication protocol should already be understood before openings begin.

What early involvement should accomplish

Hiring early is useful only if the preparation produces concrete results. Before trial, the team should know:

  • How exhibits will be requested, displayed, annotated, and removed from view.
  • Which exhibit set and numbering system controls.
  • How deposition designations, counter-designations, and rulings will be tracked.
  • How impeachment material will be retrieved quickly.
  • Which demonstratives will be used with each witness or argument.
  • What technology the courtroom provides and what must be supplied.
  • What happens if a laptop, cable, display, audio path, or file fails.
  • How last-minute revisions will be delivered and verified.

What to provide at the start

The trial technician does not need every final file on day one. A useful kickoff package usually includes the current exhibit list, available exhibit files, anticipated witness order, deposition designations, demonstratives, courtroom information, and any known presentation requirements.

A brief strategy conversation is equally important. The technician should understand the central issues, the most important documents, expected credibility disputes, technically difficult evidence, and the moments counsel believes will matter most.

Trial technician versus courtroom AV

A courtroom AV provider makes the infrastructure work: screens, projectors, audio, switching, cabling, and related equipment. A trial technician or hot-seat operator works with the legal team to make the evidence work in the presentation of the case.

Some trials need both. The distinction matters because reliable equipment does not by itself create an organized exhibit database, prepare video designations, anticipate impeachment, or understand how counsel wants to move through a witness.

Questions to ask before hiring

  • How do you prepare for exhibits, deposition video, demonstratives, and impeachment?
  • What information and deadlines do you need from the trial team?
  • How do you handle updates and conflicting exhibit versions?
  • What backup systems do you bring or recommend?
  • Have you worked with the relevant presentation formats and courtroom setup?
  • Can you provide support in the trial venue without unnecessary travel costs?

The bottom line

Defense counsel should hire a trial technician when the presentation workload begins to compete with the legal team’s ability to prepare the case. The more documents, video, demonstratives, witnesses, and technical uncertainty involved, the earlier that point arrives.

The purpose is not simply to have someone press buttons in court. It is to create a tested system that keeps the evidence available, the examination moving, and counsel focused on advocacy.
